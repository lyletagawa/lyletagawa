## Dunbar's Number Isn't a Number

Malcolm Gladwell made W.L. Gore & Associates famous in "The Tipping Point," his case study for a number pulled from primate brain scans running a multi-billion-dollar manufacturing company{{< cite 1 "Gladwell, Malcolm (2000). The Tipping Point: How Little Things Can Make a Big Difference. Little, Brown and Company." >}}. Gore capped every plant at 150 employees, and the rule came from a parking lot instead of a boardroom. "We put a hundred and fifty parking spaces in the lot, and when people start parking on the grass, we know it's time to build a new plant," a Gore associate explained. Founder Bill Gore put it more bluntly. "We found again and again that things get clumsy at a hundred and fifty{{< cite 1 "Gladwell, Malcolm (2000). The Tipping Point: How Little Things Can Make a Big Difference. Little, Brown and Company." >}}."

Nobody at Gore ran a regression analysis. The number came from Robin Dunbar, an Oxford anthropologist, and it might not be a number at all.

## What Is Dunbar's Number?

In 1992, Dunbar plotted neocortex size against group size across 36 primate genera and found a tight correlation. The bigger the thinking part of a primate's brain relative to the rest, the larger the social group it could hold together{{< cite 2 "Dunbar, Robin I. M. (1992). Neocortex Size as a Constraint on Group Size in Primates. Journal of Human Evolution, 22(6), 469-493." >}}. Extending that same regression line to the human neocortex predicted a mean group size of 148, casually rounded to 150 for the history books.

150 was never a precise figure. It was the midpoint of a 95% confidence interval running from 100 to 230, a range popular science dropped the moment Gladwell's version of the story started spreading.

## The Layers Underneath 150

Later work with Dunbar filled in what sits below it. Analyzing everything from hunter-gatherer bands to modern personal networks, Dunbar and colleagues found human social groups cluster into a discrete series of nested layers, roughly 5, 15, 50, 150, 500, and 1,500, each layer about three times the size of the one inside it{{< cite 3 "Zhou, Wei-Xing, Didier Sornette, Russell A. Hill, and Robin I. M. Dunbar (2005). Discrete Hierarchical Organization of Social Group Sizes. Proceedings of the Royal Society B, 272(1561), 439-444." >}}. Five is who you'd call at 3 a.m. Fifteen is your close friend group, fifty is people you'd invite to a big party, and a hundred fifty is meaningful contacts, people whose death you'd hear about and feel something.

Most citations of "Dunbar's number" for team size actually want the 15 layer or the 50 layer. A group of 150 people behaves like an org chart. Fifteen or fifty behaves like a team.

## Counterarguments

In 2021, three Stockholm University researchers reran Dunbar's own method on a larger, more complete primate dataset and got nowhere close to a clean 150. Two different methods gave two different answers. A Bayesian approach put the number between 69 and 109, with a 95% confidence interval of 4 to 520. A generalized least-squares approach put it between 16 and 42, with a 95% confidence interval of 2 to 336{{< cite 4 "Lindenfors, Patrik, Andreas Wartel, and Johan Lind (2021). Dunbar's Number Deconstructed. Biology Letters, 17(5)." >}}. Whichever method you trust, the uncertainty swallows the number. Lead author Patrik Lindenfors put it plainly. The theoretical foundation of Dunbar's number is shaky, and primate sociality has other, better explanations than brain size alone.

Dunbar responded directly, in his own words, in a piece for The Conversation. The dispute comes down to which regression method fits data where both variables carry measurement error. "The Stockholm study uses the most common form, least squares regression, or LSR... LSR radically underestimates the true slope when this isn't the case. For the same dataset, the LSR method used by the Stockholm study predicts 71, whereas the RMA method I originally used predicts 158{{< cite 5 "Dunbar, Robin I. M. (2021). Dunbar's Number: Why My Theory That Humans Can Only Maintain 150 Friendships Has Withstood 30 Years of Scrutiny. The Conversation." >}}." Same primate data, a more than twofold gap in the predicted number depending only on which line-fitting method you trust.

Dunbar backs his number with evidence beyond the original regression too. A survey of 43 households' Christmas card lists found an average network size of about 153{{< cite 6 "Hill, Russell A., and Robin I. M. Dunbar (2003). Social Network Size in Humans. Human Nature, 14(1), 53-72." >}}, and he points to military fighting units clustering near 150 across history, independently of anyone's neocortex math{{< cite 5 "Dunbar, Robin I. M. (2021). Dunbar's Number: Why My Theory That Humans Can Only Maintain 150 Friendships Has Withstood 30 Years of Scrutiny. The Conversation." >}}.

Both sides are arguing about method, and neither one thinks a group-size effect is imaginary. They disagree about how tightly you're allowed to state it.

## Conclusion

A cognitive limit on stable relationships that shows up in primate brain scans, Christmas card lists, and two millennia of army organization independently is a real pattern worth taking seriously. It just isn't precise enough to hang a specific policy on, and somewhere between 71 and 158 is the most defensible range anyone has produced. 150 stuck because it was easy to remember. Nobody ever nailed it down to the person.

Next time "Dunbar's number" comes up to justify a specific team size cap, ask which version is doing the work, the RMA estimate, the LSR estimate, or just the one catchy enough for a bestselling book. The pattern is sound. The precision was always borrowed.

M. (1993). Coevolution of Neocortical Size, Group Size and Language in Humans. Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 16(4), 681-694." >}}.

## Outtakes

**Even Dunbar's original number had a huge range.** His 1992 regression predicted a mean of 148, but the 95% confidence interval ran from 100 to 230. 150 is what got remembered. The range is what got dropped ([Dunbar, 1992](https://doi.org/10.1016/0047-2484(92)90081-J)).

**NPR made the counterpoint in its own headline.** "Don't Believe Facebook, You Only Have 150 Friends" ran in 2011 ([NPR, 2011](https://www.npr.org/2011/06/04/136723316/dont-believe-facebook-you-only-have-150-friends)), the same year Pew Research found the average adult Facebook user reported 229 friends ([Pew Research Center, 2011](https://www.pewresearch.org/2011/06/16/social-networking-sites-and-our-lives/)).

**The Roman army landed on the same range two thousand years earlier.** The maniple, Rome's basic tactical infantry unit, numbered about 120 soldiers, independently converging on roughly what modern militaries still use ([Wikipedia, n.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maniple_(military_unit))).
