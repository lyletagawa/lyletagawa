## Burn the boats

The morning after Cortés landed at Veracruz in 1519, his men woke to find their ships gone. Not sailed away, not lost to a storm. Run aground on his orders, stripped of sail and rigging, sunk in the shallows where everyone could see them.

Some of his own soldiers had been talking about going back to Cuba. Cortés didn't argue with them. He removed the option.

## What is burning the boats?

Nobody in Cortés's camp could sail home anymore. That was the point. Thomas Schelling, the economist who spent a career studying how people make threats and promises credible, called this a strategic move, a choice that changes what the other side expects of you by changing what you're still capable of doing{{< cite 1 "Schelling, Thomas C. (1960). The Strategy of Conflict. Harvard University Press." >}}. You don't convince anyone you'll fight to the end by saying so. You convince them by making retreat physically impossible.

Julius Caesar did the same thing nearly 1,600 years earlier, on dry land. Roman law forbade a general from bringing his army south of the Rubicon river into Italy, on pain of treason. In 49 BC, Caesar crossed it anyway, with a legion, committing himself to civil war the moment his boot touched the far bank. "The die is cast," he is said to have told his officers{{< cite 2 "Suetonius (c. 121). The Twelve Caesars, Book I: Julius Caesar." >}}. He wasn't hoping he'd win. He'd made losing and going home the same outcome.

## The move keeps getting reinvented

The tactic shows up independently across history, which suggests it solves a real problem rather than gets copied as a trick. Cortés is often remembered as burning his ships outright, but the more reliable accounts say his men ran most of them aground and stripped the wood and rigging for the camp, keeping one intact to send treasure back to Spain{{< cite 3 "HISTORY. Hernán Cortés." >}}. Whether by fire or by hull damage, the outcome was the same. Nobody was sailing back to Cuba.

A century and a half before Caesar, on the other side of the world, the Chu general Xiang Yu did something close to identical without ever hearing of the Rubicon. Before the Battle of Julu in 207 BC, he had his troops smash their cooking pots and sink the boats they'd crossed the river in, leaving three days of rations and no way back{{< cite 4 "Sima Qian (c. 94 BC). Shiji, Annals of Xiang Yu. Chinese Text Project." >}}. His outnumbered army broke the siege. The idiom his soldiers coined, "break the cauldrons, sink the boats," still means the same thing in Chinese that "cross the Rubicon" means in English.

## Make it cost something

The reason burning the boats works isn't willpower. It's economics. A promise that costs nothing to break isn't a commitment, it's cheap talk, and everyone watching knows the difference{{< cite 1 "Schelling, Thomas C. (1960). The Strategy of Conflict. Harvard University Press." >}}. Cortés could have announced that nobody was allowed to suggest retreat. His men would have kept thinking about Cuba anyway. Sinking the ships removed the thought along with the option.

The same logic runs the other direction too. Once retreat is gone, every remaining decision gets easier, because a whole category of choices, the ones that involve going back, no longer exists. That narrowing is the mechanism. Constraining your own future choices is how you make today's commitment real, to yourself as much as to anyone watching.

## Common mistakes

**You burn a bridge that costs you nothing.** Announcing something dramatic doesn't count if quietly walking it back later costs nothing too. A public deadline nobody enforces, a "no more discussion" declared in a meeting people ignore an hour later, these read as commitment devices but function as theater. If reversing the decision is still cheap, you haven't burned anything.

**You use it before you've earned the right to.** Burning the boats doesn't make a decision correct. It makes reversing it expensive. Committing hard to an untested plan just trades the ability to learn you were wrong for the appearance of conviction. The tactic is for a decision you've already stress-tested. It doesn't replace that step.

**You burn someone else's boat.** The device is meant to bind you. Using it to trap other people is a different move. Removing a team's exits without their buy-in, a leader announcing there's no plan B for a bet the team never agreed to, spends other people's optionality to manufacture your own resolve. That's cornering people.

## Put it into practice

Before you burn anything, check whether it's already been stress-tested. Burning the boats is for a decision already pressure-tested through debate, data, or a real pilot. Skipping that work and manufacturing conviction instead is a different move, and a riskier one.

Then make sure the fire is real. A commitment device only works if reversing it costs something real, in money, in reputation, in time nobody gets back. If the escape hatch is still there, quietly, all you've done is announce a decision.

If you're burning a boat that belongs to other people, tell them first. The tactic works because it forces honesty about what you're willing to risk. Spend someone else's ships and you've made a bet with their money.
